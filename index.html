Working papers

Estimating Intergenerational Effects of Early Age Human Capital Investment

Stephen O'Connell, Michael Pham, Joshua Leinwand

Working paper, August 2026.

Abstract

We estimate the true early-age human capital production function and distinguish its parameters from those perceived by parents. Following Attanasio et al. (2020), we model child human capital and parental investment as latent factors and use a control-function approach to address the endogeneity of investment. We then embed the estimated production function in an overlapping-generations model of forward-looking parental investment, drawing on Del Boca et al. (2014). Within the model, parents choose investments according to the production technology they perceive, while children’s human capital outcomes are governed by the true technology. Differences between the perceived and true parameters therefore generate systematic under- or over-investment, with consequences that may be compounded across stages of childhood and across generations. Using NLSY79 data, we estimate the parameters of the structural model by simulated method of moments and quantify the extent to which parental misperceptions distort investment choices, the distribution of child human capital, and the intergenerational evolution of human capital.

Market Structure and Cost Pass-Through in Retail Gasoline

Joshua Leinwand

Working paper, July 2026.

Abstract

In this paper I seek to understand whether the structure of a local gasoline market changes the degree to which wholesale costs are passed through into retail prices. To do so, I use 604,817 station-week observations covering 6,556 stations, first estimating station and calendar-week fixed-effects models before interacting wholesale costs with measures of local concentration. I find that above-median HHI is associated with a 0.0070 increase in pass-through, while a one-standard-deviation increase in nearby rivals is associated with a 0.0026 decrease; both estimates are significant at the 1 percent level. The independent- station share, by contrast, has no statistically significant relationship with pass-through. The dynamic results suggest that concentration changes the timing of adjustment rather than simply increasing it at every point, while a complementary margin regression finds that a 0.10 increase in HHI is associated with roughly 0.8 cents per gallon in higher margins. Thus, although these estimates are conditional relationships rather than causal merger effects, local competitive structure nevertheless appears within the pricing conduct that consumers face.

Price Effects in U.S. Merger Retrospectives: A Meta-Analytic Approach

Andrew Olsen, Reed Orchinik, Marc Remer · Working Paper · July 2026

Description

We conduct a meta-analysis of U.S. merger retrospectives, a large and growing literature that investigates ex post merger outcomes—typically price—through reduced-form methods. We first document that mergers studied are unrepresentative of the universe of U.S. mergers but are observably similar to mergers that receive antitrust scrutiny. We then synthesize the literature by computing a summary price effect using a Bayesian hierarchical model. For the subset of papers in our sample that estimate log-price effects, we find a mean price increase of 7.06%. We also develop a new meta-analytic approach that allows us to combine and analyze different outcomes affected by mergers. Using this methodology, for the entire sample of papers that study price, we find that anticompetitive outcomes are 1.7 times more likely than procompetitive outcomes. We also find that price effects are higher for healthcare and airline mergers. We maintain a companion website (https://www.mergermeta.com/) tracking developments in the merger retrospective literature and welcome submissions of new results.

An Analysis of the Connection Between the Structure of Markets and Lobbying Expenditures

Andrew Olsen, Marc Remer · Working Paper · August 2025

Description

We analyze the connection between the structure of markets and lobbying expenditures. Markets with fewer firms with greater market share tend to have higher prices and less innovation.  Traditional measures of market share and structure, however, may overstate the actual level of competition between firms.  This is because these measures do not account for the holdings of large institutional investors, such as BlackRock and Vanguard, which have ownership stakes in multiple firms in the same market. We build on recent research showing that larger ownership stakes by institutional investors within a market leads to higher prices. In particular, we will be looking at how the holdings of institutional investors within a market affects the degree to which firms lobby the federal government. To undertake the analysis, we develop a theoretical model which explains the connection between market structure, institutional investors, and the incentive to lobby the federal government. We will test predictions of the model using a large dataset on lobbying expenditures and investor holdings. Part of the empirical analysis is descriptive in nature, showing general trends in lobbying expenditure and institutional investment. We also test the causal relationship between lobbying and institutional investment, in order to see if these investors are incentivizing firms to lobby the government at the expense of consumer welfare.
